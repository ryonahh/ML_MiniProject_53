Forecasting the medal table from each country’s momentum

Our model predicts how many Olympic medals a country will win using only earlier Games. Instead of guessing the count directly, it learns how much a country rises or falls from one Games to the next.

    How the pipeline works

    Each step uses only information that existed before the Games being predicted. Unit tests check this: changing a future result must never change a past feature.

    1. Clean the records

      271,116 athlete rows from Kaggle’s 120 years of Olympic history. Summer Games only, with team medals counted once per event, as in the official tables.

    2. One row per country per Games

      Athletes, sports, events and medals for 206 countries. West and East Germany count as Germany, and the USSR as Russia.

    3. Look back, never forward

      Nine lagged features: last Games’ medals and golds, team size, sports, events, medal and athlete growth, a three-Games average, and medals per athlete.

    4. Add the World Bank

      The reference paper’s indicators: GDP, GDP per capita, growth, population, land area, and shares of world GDP and population, from the year before the Games.

    5. Learn momentum

      A Random Forest predicts the change in log medals since the last Games. The forecast adds that change back to last Games’ count.

    6. Test on the future

      Tuned on London 2012, tested on Rio 2016, backtested on six Games, then used to forecast Tokyo 2020 and scored against the real medal table.

    Why predict the change, not the count

    A Random Forest can only predict values it has already seen, averaged inside its leaves. If it learns medal counts, one leaf has to cover nations with 3 medals and nations with 90, so the big nations get pulled toward the middle.

    Learning momentum fixes this. A country that sent a bigger team and won more medals per athlete tends to grow by a similar percentage whatever its size, so one leaf can serve Kenya and China.

    How the eight models compare

    Every model uses the same Random Forest (), averaged over random seeds. Lower error is better. Gold is our model, silver the same features predicting the count, bronze the baseline.

    The paper’s models, re-run on our splits

    We re-ran each model type from the reference paper with history and World Bank features, once predicting the count and once predicting momentum. Each was tuned on 2012 and tested once on Rio 2016. Momentum lowers the error for five of the six types that can use it, including the paper’s own best pairing of Naive Bayes and Ridge. Poisson regression needs a count target, so it has no momentum version.

    The real future: Tokyo 2020

    The dataset stops at 2016, so these forecasts were made without any 2020 information and then scored against the actual medal table. Tokyo was postponed a year by COVID and hosted by Japan. Here the models with World Bank data do best: the paper’s two-stage design has the lowest error, and the economic models are clearly better on the top ten nations. Predicting momentum, our winner on every earlier Games, falls back to the middle of the pack.

    What didn’t work, and what’s left

    • A momentum score as an extra feature

      Combining medal growth, athlete growth and efficiency into one score made no difference (MAE vs ). The forest already has those three inputs. The idea only helped once momentum became the target.

    • The paper’s two-stage model

      Rebuilt with its World Bank inputs, it beats the plain count model and wins on Tokyo 2020, but it trails predicting momentum in every backtest Games.

    • SVR predicting the count

      An RBF-kernel SVR predicting the count has the best RMSE and top-ten error on Rio 2016, and momentum did not help it. This is a single test year, so we report it without reading too much into it.

    • World Bank data has gaps

      There are no figures for the Soviet Union, East Germany or Taiwan, so of rows lack economic data. On Rio 2016 the indicators barely change the error. They do help on Tokyo 2020 and on the top ten nations.

    • Shocks the data can’t see

      Host-nation effects (Japan won 58 medals in Tokyo, up from 41), boycotts and a COVID-delayed Games are outside what any of these features describe.

    • Feature importance is not cause

      Last Games’ medals and medals per athlete dominate the importance ranking. That shows what the model relies on, not why countries win.
